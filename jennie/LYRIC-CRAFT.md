# Lyric craft and QA harness

How we write and quality-check the Jennie and Gunnar wedding song. Claude runs every draft through this before showing it. Song quality comes first; the visuals adapt to the song.

Grounded in: Pat Pattison (Berklee) on prosody, object writing and show-don't-tell; standard rhyme-type theory; and two reference songs we like (KATSEYE "Animals" for tight pop economy, Taylor Swift "Love Story" for storytelling structure).

---

## The order of work (three phases)

We do these in order. Do not polish wording before the story and structure are locked.

### Phase 0 - Story and concept
- Pick ONE central metaphor. Turn the abstract feeling (love, home) into a concrete image (gravity, true north, a summit). Never say the abstract word if an image can carry it.
- One-line logline the whole song serves.
- Decide the four (max five) strong images we will actually show. Everything else is B-roll.

### Phase 1 - Drafting (bones)
- Lock the structure: Verse / Pre-Chorus / Chorus / Verse / Bridge / Final Chorus / short Outro.
- Write the CHORUS first: 4 short lines, the hook (title) as the LAST line, one idea only.
- Rough the verses as story beats, not finished lines. Get the rhyme scaffold in place.
- Correct emotional shape: joy up front, ONE dip in the bridge, key-change lift into the final chorus.
- At this phase we care about story, structure, hook, rhyme scaffold. Not perfect wording yet.

### Phase 2 - Finetuning (line by line)
- Run every line through the Rubric below.
- Cut words. Then cut more. Replace statements with images. Upgrade weak verbs.
- Fix rhymes and syllable counts for singability.
- Read it out loud in time. If a line can't be sung in one comfortable breath, it is too long.

---

## The rubric (score each line and each section)

Score each dimension 1-5. Anything at 3 or below gets rewritten. A line must pass ALL of these.

### 1. Economy (the big one)
- Max meaning, min words. Ask: can this be said in fewer words? Do it.
- ONE clear point per line. "I was chasing the sunrise, always one more place to go" = two points about the same thing = cut to one.
- Chorus lines are SHORT: aim 4-7 syllables, one breath. Long lines break into two bars and bloat the song.
- Fragments over full sentences. Lyrics are not prose. "Fingernails, one, two, three colors painted" is an image, not a sentence. Aim for that.
- Benchmark: KATSEYE "Animals" fits a whole song plus 6 choruses into 2:39 because almost nothing is a complete sentence.

### 2. Show, don't tell (imagery over statement)
- Replace abstractions (love, freedom, home, lonely) with things you can see, touch, hear.
- Anchor to concrete nouns and objects (a passport stamp, a swing, a ring, a summit flag). Object writing.
- Suggest, don't spell out. Poetic restraint: circle the feeling, let the listener land it. "Animals" implies everything and states almost nothing.
- Concrete specifics (country names, a stamp, a stove) live in the VERSES. The chorus stays universal so it can repeat.

### 3. Rhyme and prosody
- Rhyme cleanly and consistently. Non-rhyming narrative lyric only works in slow storytelling genres; for pop it must rhyme or the ear rejects it.
- Perfect/family rhyme for resolved, confident, happy lines (verses, choruses). Looser assonance/near rhyme for unstable, aching moments (the bridge). Let the rhyme type match the emotion.
- Prosody: every element supports the feeling. Confident section = steady, resolved. Aching section = sparse, unresolved.
- Verbs are amplifiers. Pick the strongest, most specific verb. "chased," "crossed," "climbed" beat "went," "was," "had."

### 4. Structure and repetition (catchiness)
- The chorus IS the song. Repeat it near-verbatim 4-6 times. Repetition, not new information, is what makes it stick.
- Hook = title, placed as the LAST line of the chorus, so the ear waits for it and gets paid off.
- Change ONLY the final chorus's words for the story payoff (the "Love Story" move); keep the melody.
- Pre-chorus = ramp that builds into the chorus. Bridge = short contrast + the emotional low, then slam into the final chorus.
- Add cheap ear-candy: a vocable or gang-vocal hook (oh-oh-oh) between sections.

### 5. Singability
- Say it out loud in tempo. Open vowels on held/high notes. No tongue-twisters or consonant pileups on the big notes.
- Natural stress must land on the beat. Read the line; where your voice naturally emphasizes should be the strong beat.

### 6. Voice and truth (project rules - non-negotiable)
- POV: the girl's, first person, direct address ("you").
- No real names. It is "a girl and a guy" who resemble them; guests connect the dots.
- Her freedom is a FLEX, never loneliness. She traveled because she was fearless and free, not because no one chose her. He is a co-adventurer she gains, not a rescue from being alone. (Exception: the long-distance bridge is honest "missing you," which is fine.)
- Pet names, POV-correct: HE calls HER "Boo-boo" (spell it Boo-boo for Suno) in the proposal; SHE calls HIM "Dudu Bear" in the last line. One reveal each, saved for those moments.
- Plain hyphens only. No em dashes or en dashes anywhere.

## What the two reference songs teach

**KATSEYE "Animals" (tight pop economy):** one idea (a hidden wild side), hammered; chorus repeats ~6x; lines are 4-6 words, images not sentences; hook word lands last every time; clean simple rhymes locked on one vowel; counting and vocables as ear-candy; specifics are few and sharp.

**Taylor Swift "Love Story" (storytelling structure):** clean rhyme throughout is what lets a narrative still feel pop; title hook in every chorus; ONE archetype (Romeo/Juliet) does the work of a hundred details; chronological story; bridge is the low, right before the payoff; key change on the final chorus; the last chorus rewrites its words to resolve the story.

**Our target:** as tight and economical as "Animals," with a little more of "Love Story"'s storytelling. Not more complex than that.
